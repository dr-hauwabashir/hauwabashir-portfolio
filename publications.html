Research

Health, women and the news

Health communication and women’s health, and how newsrooms and journalism schools decide whose voices count.

2019–2026 PhD research Cyprus International University Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Dilan Çiftçi Co-supervisor: Asst. Prof. Dr. Bahar Taşeli

Whose voices make the news in a health crisis

The problem

In a health emergency, newspapers stand between health authorities and the public. Earlier studies of Nigerian coverage mostly looked at the first weeks of COVID-19 and described sources, tone and format one at a time.

What I did

I coded 467 articles from five national dailies, Daily Trust, Leadership, Punch, The Nation and ThisDay, from March 2020 to March 2021, on seven variables, and analysed them in SPSS. Alongside it I wrote a scoping review of research on COVID-19 and women’s reproductive health.

What happened

Official sources dominated 88.2% of articles. Coverage built on officials was more often positive in tone, and coverage about ordinary people was mostly negative. Of 102 articles centred on ordinary people, only 4 rested mainly on unofficial sources. The newspapers worked as a relay for official statements, and much less as a forum where those statements were tested.

What I would do differently

All the coding was mine. A second coder would make the findings on tone and on who was at the centre of each story more secure. I would also record every source in an article, not only the main one, and separate federal, state and public health officials.
